1. Project Title
ARIA — Autonomous Reasoning & Interaction Agent. A cognitive, multi-agent vision-languageaction software stack for adaptive manipulation planning.

2. Abstract
ARIA is a locally-run, multi-agent manipulation system built around an affordable 5-DoF arm. It understands
spoken or typed instructions, works through them step by step, and picks up, moves, and manipulates objects
roughly the way a person would: it adjusts for uncertainty, checks in with the user when it isn't sure, and
keeps getting better at its own skills over time.

3. Full Project Description
ARIA runs on the simulation of a 5-degree-of-freedom arm: SG90 simulated servos handle the lighter
joints, and MG995 simulated servos units take the load-bearing ones. Vision comes from either an ESP32CAM simultion or a Logitech C270 like camera node, and an MPU6050 IMU simulated sensor sits on
the end effector to supply orientation feedback the camera alone can't give. Commands arrive as natural
language and get broken down through a fixed hierarchy: command, goal, subgoals, actions. Every step is
written out as a plain-language chain of thought, for example locate the object, locate the target, plan the
grasp, execute it, verify it, move, release, and confirm completion.
Perception is deliberately hybrid rather than purely learned. YOLO handles fast per-frame detection, while
monocular depth from Depth-Anything or MiDaS is combined with straightforward camera and table-height
calibration geometry to produce a reliable Z coordinate. That combination matters because there's no
dedicated depth sensor anywhere in this setup. Grasp candidates come from GraspNet or AnyGrasp and then
get filtered by an Affordance Agent, which tries to pick the grasp that matches how an object is actually used
rather than just the nearest reachable surface. Motion runs through whichever inverse-kinematics solver tests
out best for the arm's geometry (ikpy, roboticstoolbox-python, an analytical solution, trac-ik, KDL, or a neural
IK model), executed through MoveIt2 with cubic or quintic spline trajectories, and planned with awareness
of both workspace obstacles and the arm's own trailing cables.
Decisions come from an LLM-based Planning Agent, ideally run locally, using function-calling to hand work
off to the right specialist agent. Optional Tree-of-Thought or Monte Carlo Tree Search exploration kicks in
when a task has real branching complexity. Every action carries a confidence score, and when that confidence
drops low enough, the system stops and asks the user before proceeding instead of guessing through
uncertainty silently. A World Model Agent keeps a running semantic map of known objects, including their
coordinates, color and material attributes, and a lifecycle state that moves through detected, tracked, lost,
recovered, moved, and removed. If an object isn't where the map says it should be, ARIA checks with the
user rather than assuming it simply failed to see it.
Underneath all of that, the system keeps watching its own health (servo temperature, mechanical drift,
calibration drift, camera frame rate, inference latency) and its own performance (localisation error, IK error,
pick success rate, task completion rate, planning and inference time, recovery rate). That data feeds a
Learning Agent, which gradually improves a fixed library of skills (pick, place, push, pull, stack, sort, inspect,
slide, roll, sweep), and an Evaluation Agent, which sorts failures into useful categories: missed object, object
slipped, IK failure, collision, or perception error, so troubleshooting has somewhere to start. A separate learn
mode lets someone teleoperate the arm directly, logging images, joint states, and commands for later use in
improving autonomous behavior.

5. Where the Novelty Actually Is
None of the individual pieces here are new on their own. YOLO, depth estimation, GraspNet, MoveIt2, and
open VLA policies like LeRobot or OpenVLA already exist and get used across robotics research. What this
project actually proposes is a particular way of wiring all of it together, so that a $150–200 arm can behave
like something closer to a research-grade platform, done with an architecture that stays legible enough to
debug. A few things make the combination worth calling out specifically.
• Agent-hierarchy scale. Around fifteen cooperating agents (vision, depth, tracking, affordance, planning,
skill, control, safety, memory, world model, learning, evaluation, dialogue, attention, reachability) run
together on a single consumer GPU, which most published multi-agent robotics stacks don't attempt.
• Hybrid perception. Classical camera and table-height calibration geometry combines with learned
monocular depth to recover a trustworthy Z coordinate, useful when the only camera on hand is an
ESP32-CAM or a webcam with no native depth sensing.
• Affordance-and-confidence loop. Grasps get chosen based on how an object is actually used, a cup by
its handle, a tool by its shaft, and the system only pauses to ask the user when its own confidence
estimate says it should.
• Self-supervised operational logging. IK error, pick success rate, recovery rate, and localisation drift
feed straight into a Learning Agent that improves the skill library over time.
• Explainability. An explicit chain-of-thought log runs for every task, alongside cable-aware,
self-calibrating motion planning on hobby servos, neither of which is common at this price point.
Taken individually, each of these is incremental. Put together as a system, though, they add up to something
that behaves noticeably more capable than the sum of its parts would suggest.

6. Use Cases
• Research and teaching. A way for students and small labs to work with embodied AI, multi-agent
coordination, and vision-language-action models without needing an industrial arm or an expensive
RGB-D rig.
• Desktop and lab automation. Sorting small parts, organizing tools, handling lab consumables that don't
need much force.
• Assistive-manipulation prototyping. Testing whether an arm can fetch everyday objects like cups,
bottles, or tools on a spoken command, before committing to more expensive hardware.
• Benchmarking testbed. Running the same task across different IK solvers, depth models, or VLA
policies to see which actually performs best on identical hardware.
• Human-robot interaction research. The confidence-gated approval workflow and chain-of-thought
logging make this a decent platform for studying trust and explainability.
• Dexterity research. In-hand manipulation goals such as rotating a screwdriver or repositioning a
paintbrush give it a natural angle into fine manipulation.

7. Benefits
•​ Lower cost of entry. SG90/MG995 servos and a webcam instead of an industrial arm and a dedicated
depth camera bring the price down by an order of magnitude or more.
•​ Explainability. Chain-of-thought logging makes every decision traceable, which helps with debugging
and is genuinely useful for teaching.
•​ Safer operation. The confidence-gated approval step and a dedicated Safety Agent reduce the odds of
damaging an object or the arm itself.
•​ Continuous improvement. Logged success rate, IK error, and recovery rate give the Learning Agent
something concrete to work from, without a full retraining cycle.
•​ Modularity. The clean separation between the arm, perception, and planner layers means the same stack
could, in principle, be pointed at a different arm with only modest rework.
•​ Graceful degradation. The system actively looks for better viewing angles, searches the workspace
when something's missing, and tries to recover from failures rather than just stopping.

12. Future Scope
•​ 6D pose estimation (FoundationPose or MegaPose): adds orientation awareness for tasks like inserting a
key or connecting a plug, something the current X/Y/Z-only pipeline can't do.
•​ SAM2 segmentation in place of YOLO's bounding boxes: pixel-accurate masks should improve grasp
planning and handling of partially occluded objects.
•​ 3D Gaussian Splatting reconstruction of the workspace, letting the system reason about the scene
persistently, without re-running a full depth pass every frame.
•​ Material recognition feeding directly into grasp force, so the arm grips glass gently and a metal tool
firmly.
•​ Containerized deployment, CI/CD with simulated regression tests, and experiment tracking (Weights &
Biases or similar), to make the system easier to maintain and compare against itself as it changes.
•​ Scaling up from one arm to a multi-arm or mobile-base setup, backed by a growing database of known
objects with their CAD models, materials, and learned affordances.

13. Drawbacks and Honest Limitations
•​ Hardware ceiling. SG90 and MG995 servos have backlash, drift, and limited torque compared to
industrial actuators, and no amount of software will fully compensate for that.
•​ Compute risk. Running VLA models, depth estimation, segmentation, and an LLM planner all at once
on a single RTX 5060 will need careful scheduling and probably some model quantization to stay within
real-time budgets.
•​ Sensing limits. A webcam or ESP32-CAM paired with monocular depth is less accurate than a proper
stereo or RGB-D setup, especially on transparent, reflective, or very thin objects.
•​ System complexity. Fifteen-plus cooperating agents, several IK solver options, several VLA backends,
and two depth models add up to a lot of surface area for a small team to keep working correctly at once.
•​ Latency stacking. By the time an image has gone through detection, depth, grasp planning, IK, and
trajectory generation, the accumulated delay may be too slow for something genuinely fast-moving, like
a rolling pencil.
•​ No dedicated force sensor. Force estimation is inherently approximate, which is a real limitation for
anything fragile or high-force.
